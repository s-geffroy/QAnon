Q's predictions

Q made countless announcements: arrests, indictments, declassifications, an imminent "storm". To list them all and check them against the facts, every drop is submitted to a language model, chosen after an evaluation on a reference annotation set.

Did the predictions come true?

250 specific or dated predictions checked one by one. Of the 140 checkable ones, 39 came true, 22 partly and 79 did not (110 unverifiable), a fulfilment rate of 28%. 35 concerned events already publicly scheduled. Excluding them, the rate is 16%. 40 automatic extractions were not real predictions.

Method

The horizon is the stated deadline, otherwise 12 months after the drop, and every checkable prediction gets a sourced verdict. "Trivial" means an event already announced or scheduled when Q posted (election, hearing, official trip). When Q adopts someone else's announcement, the quote counts as Q's prediction (trivial if already public), but a quote made to denounce, or not about the future, does not. Rate = fulfilled ÷ checkable (fulfilled + partial + not fulfilled). The full protocol is in verification-README.md (Data). Verification was assisted by research agents, with sources consulted online, and is still to be reviewed.

The horoscope test

Q claimed the news confirmed his posts ("Future proves past"). But a vague text "fits" almost anything, the Barnum effect known from horoscopes. In the test, a drop and two lists of news items, the real period and a randomly drawn one, are shown blind, with no date at all, and a language model must say which one the drop refers to.

With the news of the 7 days before the drop, the model picks the real period in 75% of decided cases. With the 30 days after, in 68%. It sees no specific link in 76% of the "future" comparisons, and 6% of the randomly drawn lists still get a score of at least 5 out of 10.

In 76% of the "future" comparisons the model finds no specific link between the drop and the news. When it does find one, the real period wins, for the past as for the future, and the two rates do not differ significantly (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.44). The "future" signal is not enough to conclude that the drops anticipate events. It concerns only a minority of comparisons, randomly drawn periods "match" too, and it is compatible with stories simply continuing from one week to the next, a hypothesis this test does not measure. The examples below show how a random period can seem to "match", either because the drop is vague enough to fit an event years later, or because it mentions an earlier fact that appears in the list.

As a robustness check, a second model (gpt-6.1-sol, best F1 in the evaluation on our reference set) answered the same questions for 100 drops. It is far more sceptical. It sees no specific link in 95 of the 100 "future" comparisons, against 70 for the main model on the same drops, and decides only 5 times (4 in favour of the real period). The two models make the same choice in 75% of cases, but their agreement beyond chance is modest (Cohen's kappa 0.23), so the few "matches" depend on the reader. This is a limit of the measurement rather than further evidence, and it rules out reading them as recognisable foresight.

Method

300 randomly drawn drops of at least 15 words (stratified by year, Nov 2017 – Nov 2020) are compared with US-related news items from Wikipedia's "Current events" portal, at most 40 per list and as many in each list. The random period lies more than six months from the drop, list order is randomised, and the model is gpt-5.6-luna with sceptical instructions (vague wording does not count). Binomial test against 50% among decided cases. If the drops foretold the future, the real "future" period would be chosen well over half the time. But news continues from week to week, so a rate above 50% may also reflect that continuity, hence the comparison with the past.
